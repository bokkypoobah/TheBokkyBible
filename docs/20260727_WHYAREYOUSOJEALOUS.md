#### Detailed Extract/Summary from "There Was Never a Place for You" (Maledicta | Marina Karlova, uploaded ~July 26, 2026)

The video is a sharp, philosophical/psychological critique of how societal roles are imposed from birth, turning living people into rigid "slots" that suppress authentic selfhood. It argues that the chronic feeling of not belonging, inadequacy, or needing to "fix" oneself stems not from personal failure but from a structural mismatch: the world was never built with space for unscripted, living humans—only for performers of assigned roles.

**Core Argument (paraphrased/condensed from transcript):**
- From infancy, you're assigned roles (child → daughter/son → student → worker → citizen → etc.) with built-in duties, scripts, emotional restrictions, and power imbalances. A "child" owes gratitude, performance, and future justification for existence—unlike a pet. These aren't neutral; they demand you earn personhood.
- Roles eventually *replace* the person. Society becomes inhumane because people relate to categories/scripts, not living beings (no empathy for a "role"). Examples: a mother treating her adult child as still-helpless to preserve her own "mother" identity; checklists over open questions.
- Early childhood wiring is key—the brain builds around these roles. Doubting or hurting leads to "you're ungrateful/fine," installing permanent shame as default. You didn't choose this; it was poured in before you had a voice.
- Result: Persistent sense of being "too sensitive/broken/different," endless self-discipline to "deserve a place." But the structure has **no slot for a living person**—only cut-down versions that fit the role. Living things don't fit without self-amputation.
- Broader system: Roles are interdependent and self-reinforcing (mother needs daughter-performer; teacher needs student). People internalize and enforce them on others.
- Resolution hint: Recognize the machine, then work on separating roles from the underlying person and restoring suppressed parts (author's ongoing methodology).

Tone is direct, unflinching, and liberating—naming the "flat structural cause" beneath personal shame without sugarcoating the difficulty of unlearning it.
