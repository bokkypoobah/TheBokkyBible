**Detailed extract from “How These Kali Maa Cigarettes SHOOK An Empire🔥| The Untold Story of India's Independence”** (YouTube video appearing yesterday morning, focused on the late-19th/early-20th-century Swadeshi-era Kali brand cigarettes and their role in economic resistance against British colonial dominance).

The core story centres on “Kali Cigarettes” (also referred to as Kali Maa Cigarettes or Bishuddho Swadeshi Kali Cigarettes), produced by the East India Cigarette Manufacturing Company (an Indo-Turkish joint venture based in Calcutta/Kolkata, at addresses such as 131 Harrison Road). These were marketed as pure indigenous/Swadeshi products in the late 1880s–1890s (with popularity extending into the early 20th century, especially around the 1905 Partition of Bengal and the broader Swadeshi movement).

Key historical points from the documented advertisements and context:

- A colour lithograph advertisement (c. 1885–90, printed by the Calcutta Art Studio, Bowbazar Street, Calcutta; a copy held in the British Museum) depicts Goddess Kali and promotes the cigarettes as “Pure Swadeshi.”
- Bangla text on the ads translates roughly as: “Kali Cigarettes. Pure Swadeshi. Presented by the East India Cigarette Manufacturing Company… If you care for the development of ‘svadeshi’ (home-grown in India) products, if you feel responsible for the poor, miserable, working people of this land, if you can truly distinguish between good and evil, then, o Hindu brothers, you must use these ‘Kali’ cigarettes!”
- Claims included that the paper was uniquely non-irritating (no coughs or respiratory issues) and superior to foreign imports.
- This was part of the wider Swadeshi economic boycott strategy: rejecting British-manufactured goods (including imported cigarettes from brands like those of Mark Polo) in favour of Indian-made alternatives to starve the colonial economy of revenue and assert self-reliance. Kali, the fierce mother goddess who destroys demons and protects her children, was deliberately invoked as the face of resistance—her image on cigarette packets turning a commercial product into a symbol of national awakening, courage, and anti-colonial defiance.
- Revolutionaries in Bengal also named homemade bombs “Kali bombs.” The British reportedly grew uneasy enough with the powerful iconography (some versions showing Kali with Englishmen’s heads) that later restrictions appeared on such imagery.
- Parallel products existed (Ganesh beedis, etc.), but Kali cigarettes stood out for explicitly linking devotion, commerce, and political liberation. They predate and feed into the larger independence narrative: economic sovereignty as a foundation for political freedom, with ordinary daily acts (choosing what to smoke) becoming quiet acts of empire-shaking.

The video frames this as an “untold” angle on how cultural-spiritual symbols + grassroots consumer resistance helped undermine British economic control long before 1947, contributing to the eventual collapse of Company/Raj dominance.
